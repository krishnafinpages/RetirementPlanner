      How the success probability is calculated

      The question it answers: if markets turn out differently from your single average-return assumption, in how many futures does your plan still pay for your spending every year through your plan-to age?

      1. 1Start from your current inputs
        Balances, spending, Social Security, pensions, Roth strategy, withdrawal order and tax rules are exactly the ones used everywhere else in the tool.
      2. 2Create 1,000 possible market paths
        Each year of each path gets a random investment return centered on your growth inputs (before and after retirement) with the yearly volatility you set in section 5. Returns come from a bell curve and each year is independent of the others.
      3. 3Run your whole plan on every path
        Taxes, required distributions, Social Security taxation, IRMAA, Roth conversions and withdrawals are recalculated year by year on each path, so poor early returns also change taxes and withdrawals.
      4. 4Count the paths that stay funded
        A path is funded when spending is met in every year and the portfolio never runs out. Success probability is funded paths divided by 1,000.
      5. 5Draw the fan and the outcome table
        For every year, the 10th, 25th, median, 75th and 90th percentile of total assets across the 1,000 paths form the shaded fan. The dashed line is the single-path projection used elsewhere in the tool.
      How to read it
      • 90% or more is Strong, 75 to 89% Moderate, 60 to 74% At risk, and below 60% High risk. These are rules of thumb, not guarantees.
      • The median outcome is lower than the single-path projection because ups and downs reduce compound growth ("volatility drag").
      • The levers table reruns the same market paths with one change at a time (lower spending, later retirement, Social Security at 70), so differences come from the change and not from luck. Those rows use the first 500 paths for speed.
      Why the same answer every time

      The random numbers use a fixed starting seed, so identical inputs always give identical results. Changing any input, including volatility, produces a new run.

      What it does not include
      • Inflation is constant and cash earns its fixed rate in every path.
      • Returns are normally distributed with no crashes beyond what that allows, and no link between inflation and returns.
      • The stress-test input is ignored here, because the simulation already covers market risk.
      • Spending stays at your goal; the model does not cut spending in bad markets, so real-life flexibility would raise the odds.
      • Tax law is held constant, and long-term care or other large surprises are not modeled.
      Choosing volatility

      As a rough guide, a conservative mix is about 6 to 8%, a balanced mix about 10 to 12% (the default), and a stock-heavy mix 15 to 18%. Use the Volatility box in section 5, Rates & Stress Test.

      Assumptions & Notes

      Tax law as of : 2026 tax year. The $6,000 senior deduction ends after 2028. RMDs follow SECURE 2.0 (age 73, or 75 if born 1960 or later).
      Inflation/COLA () is applied to: spending goal, Social Security and pension COLA, tax brackets, standard deductions, capital gains and IRMAA thresholds (IRMAA surcharges grow 1 pt faster). Not inflated: Social Security tax thresholds, NIIT thresholds and the senior deduction.
      Investment income is taxed: cash interest at ordinary rates; qualified dividends (2% brokerage yield) and brokerage gains at 0/15/20% (0% when taxable income is low) plus the 3.8% NIIT.
      Federal tax only: state income tax is not included and varies by state. See the Tax Foundation or a state-by-state retirement tax guide.
